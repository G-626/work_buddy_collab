# Therapy Report Research for CaseCraft
## Professional Report Structures for ASD/Developmental Support
### Research compiled for: Agent Creativity Hackathon (WorkBuddy Track), August 2026

---

## Executive Summary

This research synthesizes professional therapy documentation practices across occupational therapy (OT), speech-language pathology (SLP), and psychology for communicating ASD/developmental progress to parents and teachers. Key finding: **the most effective reports prioritize actionable strategies over clinical data, use a strengths-based narrative frame, and tailor content specifically to the audience (parent vs. teacher vs. clinical record).**

---

## 1. Common Documentation Formats

### 1.1 SOAP Notes (Standard Clinical Framework)
*Used by: All disciplines. Verified via Wikipedia: the SOAP note originated from Lawrence Weed's Problem-Oriented Medical Record (POMR), now the universal clinical documentation standard.*

**S — Subjective**
- Parent/caregiver report: "Mum reports Johnny had a difficult morning transition..."
- Child's self-report (when verbal): what the child says about their experience
- Relevant home/school updates since last session
- For ASD: often includes parent report of behaviors, sleep, eating, sensory regulation at home

**O — Objective**
- Quantifiable observations: repetitions completed, accuracy percentages, time on task
- Standardized assessment scores (percentile ranks, standard scores)
- Observed behaviors (not interpreted): "Johnny maintained eye contact for 3-5 seconds during turn-taking activity"
- For ASD: joint attention instances, initiation counts, response latency, sensory-seeking/avoiding behaviors observed

**A — Assessment**
- Therapist's clinical interpretation of progress toward goals
- Analysis of what's working and what isn't
- Pattern identification: "When sensory input is provided before fine motor tasks, accuracy improves ~40%"
- For ASD: functional impact analysis — how skills gained translate to daily life

**P — Plan**
- Next session focus
- Modifications to treatment approach
- Home program assignments
- For ASD: specific strategies for generalization across settings

### 1.2 IEP Progress Reports (School-Based/US Model)
*Used by: School-based OTs, SLPs, special education teachers. Universally required under IDEA.*

Standard structure:
- **Student Information** (name, grade, date of report, case manager)
- **Present Levels of Performance** (PLOP/PLAAFP) — narrative of current functioning
- **Goal-by-Goal Progress Summary** (each IEP goal listed with progress rating)
- **Progress Codes**: 
  - *Making sufficient progress to meet goal*
  - *Making some progress; may not meet goal*
  - *Making minimal/insufficient progress*
  - *Goal met*
  - *Not yet introduced*
- **Data/Evidence** (specific metrics supporting each rating)
- **Service Delivery Summary** (minutes provided vs. prescribed)
- **Recommendations** (goal modifications, continued/discontinued services)

### 1.3 Narrative Progress Summaries (Parent-Facing)
*Used by: Private practice OTs, SLPs, psychologists for parent communication. Preferred format for parent reports.*

Standard structure:
- **Session Overview** (period covered, attendance summary)
- **Strengths & Celebrations** (what went well, wins, progression moments)
- **Goal Progress by Domain** (organized by developmental area, not by clinical goal number)
- **Current Functional Levels** (described in everyday language)
- **Strategies That Worked** (the most actionable section — what parents/teachers can replicate)
- **Areas for Continued Focus** (next priorities, not "deficits")
- **Home & School Recommendations** (concrete, 3-5 specific strategies)
- **Next Steps / Look Ahead**

### 1.4 Session Notes (Short-Form, Internal)
*Used for: quick documentation between detailed reports, typically 1 paragraph per session.*

Structure: "Today we worked on [activity/skill] using [approach/strategy]. [Child] demonstrated [specific observation]. [Response to intervention]. Continue with [plan]."

## 2. Discipline-Specific Report Sections

### 2.1 Occupational Therapy (OT) Report to Parents
*Focus: fine motor, sensory processing, self-care/ADLs, visual-motor integration, executive function*

| Section | Content | Example Heading |
|---------|---------|-----------------|
| Session Overview | Attendance, general engagement level, session theme | "What We Worked On This Month" |
| Sensory Regulation | Sensory diet effectiveness, regulation strategies used, arousal level patterns | "Sensory Check-In" |
| Fine Motor Progress | Grasp patterns, in-hand manipulation, bilateral coordination, handwriting | "Hand Skills Update" |
| Self-Care / ADLs | Dressing, feeding, toileting, hygiene independence | "Growing Independence" |
| Visual-Motor / Visual-Perceptual | Cutting, drawing, puzzles, letter formation, spatial awareness | "Eye-Hand Coordination" |
| Executive Function | Task initiation, organization, planning, attention, flexibility | "Thinking & Planning Skills" |
| Strategies for Home | 3-5 concrete sensory/positioning/activity ideas with simple materials | "Try This at Home" |
| Equipment/Resources | Adaptive tools, visual schedules, sensory tools recommended | "Helpful Tools" |

### 2.2 Speech-Language Pathology (SLP) Report to Parents
*Focus: expressive/receptive language, articulation, pragmatics/social communication, AAC*

| Section | Content | Example Heading |
|---------|---------|-----------------|
| Communication Wins | Notable moments, new words/phrases, spontaneous communication | "Communication Highlights" |
| Receptive Language | Following directions, understanding concepts, comprehension growth | "Understanding Language" |
| Expressive Language | Vocabulary growth, sentence length, grammar, narrative skills | "Expressing Ideas" |
| Speech Sound Production | Articulation accuracy, intelligibility, phonological patterns | "Speech Clarity" |
| Social Communication / Pragmatics | Turn-taking, topic maintenance, perspective-taking, nonverbal cues | "Social Communication" |
| AAC Update (if applicable) | Device usage, new vocabulary programmed, independence level | "Communication Device Update" |
| Strategies for Home & School | Language modeling techniques, question types to use, communication temptations | "Boost Communication at Home" |
| Book/App Recommendations | Specific resources matched to current goals | "Recommended Resources" |

### 2.3 Psychology / Behaviour Support Report to Parents
*Focus: emotional regulation, behaviour, social skills, cognitive development, adaptive functioning*

| Section | Content | Example Heading |
|---------|---------|-----------------|
| Emotional Wellbeing | Mood, anxiety indicators, emotional vocabulary, coping skills | "How [Child] Is Feeling" |
| Behaviour Observations | Triggers identified, replacement behaviours developing, de-escalation effectiveness | "Understanding Behaviour" |
| Social Skills Progress | Peer interactions, friendship skills, perspective-taking, conflict resolution | "Making Friends" |
| Cognitive/Learning Update | Attention span, problem-solving, memory, academic readiness | "Learning & Thinking" |
| Adaptive Skills | Daily routines, transitions, flexibility, independence | "Everyday Skills" |
| Parent Support | Parent-implemented strategies that are working, coaching tips | "Supporting You" |
| School Collaboration | Teacher communication, classroom strategies tried | "Working with School" |
| Wellbeing Strategies | Emotion regulation activities, calming corner ideas, social stories used | "Wellbeing Toolkit" |

## 3. Parent Report vs. Teacher Report: Key Differences

### Parent Report (Home Focus)
- **Tone**: Warm, supportive, empowering — "you are the expert on your child"
- **Language**: Everyday terms, minimal jargon, define any clinical terms used
- **Focus**: Generalization to home routines, family quality of life, practical strategies for daily life
- **Key Question Answered**: "What should I do at home to support my child?"
- **Length**: 1-3 pages, visually clean, with bullet points and highlights
- **Emphasis**: Strengths first, progress over time, hope and trajectory
- **Unique sections**: Strategies for home routines (mealtimes, bedtime, outings), sibling interactions, family wellbeing

### Teacher Report (Classroom Focus)
- **Tone**: Professional, collaborative, solution-oriented
- **Language**: Can include educational terminology, reference curriculum standards
- **Focus**: Classroom participation, learning access, peer interactions, environmental modifications
- **Key Question Answered**: "How do I support this student in my classroom?"
- **Length**: 1-2 pages, organized by classroom domain
- **Emphasis**: Observable classroom behaviours, accommodations that work, differentiation strategies
- **Unique sections**: Classroom seating/positioning, learning environment modifications, differentiation strategies, IEP goal alignment, paraprofessional support needs, transition supports

### Combined/Coordinated Report (CaseCraft Opportunity!)
- Both parent and teacher get a version tailored to their context
- Shared understanding of goals across settings
- Consistent strategies reinforce skill generalization
- Reduces "therapist as middleman" — empowers direct parent-teacher collaboration

## 4. Professional Tone & Terminology

### Do Use (Strengths-Based, Person-First)
- "Is developing..." / "Is working toward..." / "Is beginning to..."
- "Benefits from..." / "Responds well to..." / "Thrives when..."
- "Demonstrates strength in..." / "Shows emerging skills in..."
- "With [support/strategy], [child] can..."
- "Next step in development..."
- "[Child]'s communication/regulation/motor profile..."

### Avoid (Deficit-Focused, Clinical Cold)
- "Cannot..." / "Fails to..." / "Refuses to..."
- "Deficient in..." / "Delayed compared to..."
- "Problem behaviors" → "Behaviours we're understanding better"
- "Non-compliant" → "Benefits from choices/visual supports"
- "Low-functioning" → describe specific support needs

## 5. HK / Asia Context

### HK Therapy Landscape
- **Major providers**: Heep Hong Society (協康會), SAHK (香港耀能協會), Watchdog, CDC (Child Assessment Centres under Hospital Authority/DH), private clinics
- **Common report types in HK**:
  - CDC assessment reports (multidisciplinary diagnostic reports — very clinical, used for service eligibility)
  - School-based OT/SLP progress reports (for aided schools with on-site therapy)
  - Private therapy centre progress reports (monthly/quarterly, parent-facing)
  - EETC/OPRS (Early Education and Training Centre / On-site Pre-school Rehabilitation Services) progress reports — government-subsidized, use standardized templates
- **Bilingual expectations**: Reports in HK are typically in Chinese (Traditional) for local parents, sometimes bilingual. Key sections often have both English and Chinese headings. English-dominated reports are common for international schools and expatriate families.
- **Cultural considerations**:
  - HK parents often value academic readiness indicators highly
  - Direct "homework" framing works better than "play-based activities" for some HK parents
  - Face-to-face verbal explanation alongside written report is expected
  - Teachers in HK mainstream schools need very concrete, classroom-ready strategies (time-pressed environment, large class sizes)
  - Use of "training" (訓練) terminology is common and accepted in HK (vs. Western preference for "support" / "intervention")

### China/Asia-Wide Patterns
- Growing adoption of VB-MAPP, ABLLS-R, and PEP-3 for progress tracking in autism intervention
- Increased use of standardized progress reporting influenced by international school accreditation requirements
- Teletherapy during/post-COVID has increased demand for clear written parent communication

## 6. What Makes a Report ACTUALLY Useful to Parents (Not a Checkbox Exercise)

### Research-Backed Principles

**1. Actionability Over Information Density**
Parents value "what can I do" far more than detailed clinical data. The most-remembered part of any report is the strategies section. Reports that lack concrete home activities feel like a waste of time.

**2. Strengths Before Challenges**
Leading with strengths establishes the parent as a partner, not a receiver of bad news. Frame challenges as "next steps" or "areas we're working on together" — never as failures or deficits.

**3. Visual Evidence**
Photos of the child engaged in activities, simple graphs showing progress over time, and visual schedules parents can use are more impactful than paragraphs of text. A "before and after" work sample (e.g., handwriting, drawing) communicates progress instantly.

**4. Celebrating Small Wins**
For children with significant support needs, progress can be slow. Naming specific small wins ("held a crayon with a tripod grasp for 30 seconds") validates the effort of both child and parent.

**5. Specific, Not Generic, Strategies**
"Practice fine motor at home" is useless. "Have your child use tongs to sort cotton balls into an ice cube tray while you prepare dinner — this builds the same pincer grasp needed for pencil control" is gold.

**6. Validation of Parent Experience**
Acknowledging that the parent's observations are valuable and that parenting a neurodivergent child is complex. Phrases like "As you've noticed..." or "You mentioned that..." make the report feel collaborative.

**7. Clear Trajectory**
Parents want to know: "Is my child making progress? What's next? When should I worry?" Reports should answer these questions explicitly, even if the answer is uncertain.

**8. Language Match**
Parents with limited health literacy or non-native English speakers (HK context!) need reports in accessible language. Jargon creates distance; plain language builds partnership. In HK, offering the report in the family's preferred language (Chinese or English) is critical.

### Red Flags (What Makes Reports Useless)
- Copy-paste templates with the child's name swapped in (parents can tell)
- Pure clinical data with no interpretation ("standard score 76, percentile 5")
- No home strategies whatsoever
- Only listing what the therapist did, not what the child achieved
- Deficit-focused language throughout
- Late delivery (report arriving weeks after the reporting period)
- No invitation for parent questions or feedback
- No mention of the child as a whole person (interests, personality, preferences)

## 8. Key Recommendations for CaseCraft

1. **Adopt a strengths-based, parent-friendly narrative format** (Template A) rather than a clinical SOAP format for the parent guide. Parents need the "Try This at Home" section more than raw data.

2. **Keep the teacher guide action-oriented** (Template B) — teachers read these in 5 minutes between classes. Bullet points, tables, and classroom-ready strategies win over dense prose.

3. **Include a "Strengths & Celebrations" section** in every report. This is the single most appreciated section by parents and changes the entire tone.

4. **Generate at least 3-5 specific, implemented-today strategies per report.** Generic advice ("practice at home") is worse than no advice because it makes parents feel inadequate when they don't know HOW to practice.

5. **Bilingual support**: For HK, generate parent-facing content in Traditional Chinese with option for English. Key headings should appear in both languages. Use the Chinese terminology table in Section 4.

6. **The social story is a high-value addition** — it bridges the parent-teacher communication gap and gives both parties a shared tool. It's also uniquely suited to AI generation.

7. **Avoid over-clinical tone** in parent reports. The CaseCraft system should use a friendly, conversational register for parents ("You might notice..." not "Caregivers are advised to monitor..."), while maintaining professional precision for teacher reports.

8. **Include a "Looking Ahead" section** — parents consistently report that knowing what's next reduces anxiety and helps them contextualize current progress.
